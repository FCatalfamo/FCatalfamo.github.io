## Overview

Political donations are usually studied as tools for influencing elections, lobbying policymakers, or securing access to officeholders. This paper considers an earlier and less visible channel: intervention in internal party leadership contests.

The central argument is simple. Party leaders shape agenda-setting, legislative coordination, and the direction of party policy. Donors therefore have an incentive to intervene before policy is made, by supporting the candidate whose preferences are closest to their own.

## The model

Two candidates compete for a leadership position and differ in both their policy preferences and their pre-existing leadership capital. A donor chooses whether, and how much, to contribute to either candidate. The caucus then selects a leader, taking into account candidates' baseline strength and the fundraising signals generated during the contest.

Contributions do not buy policy directly. Instead, they affect the perceived viability of candidates and thereby the probability that each one becomes leader. The selected leader subsequently implements her preferred policy.

## Main implications

The model produces three main results:

- Donors target their contributions rather than splitting support between candidates: they back the candidate whose policy position is closer to their own.
- Contributions are most valuable when ideological stakes are high and when caucus members are responsive to fundraising as a signal of viability.
- Even without lobbying legislators or influencing individual votes, donor intervention shifts expected policy outcomes toward donor preferences by increasing the likelihood that an aligned candidate wins the leadership contest.

Leadership selection is therefore an important but understudied institutional channel through which financial resources can shape political outcomes.
