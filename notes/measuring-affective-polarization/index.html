Measuring affective polarization across party systems

Some thoughts on comparative measurement and the challenges involved in comparing partisan affect across multiparty systems.

15 August 2026 6 min read
Affective Polarization Comparative Politics

Affective polarization is straightforward to describe in a two-party system: citizens feel warmly toward their own side and negatively toward the opposing side. In multiparty systems, the same idea becomes more complicated.

Voters may dislike some out-parties intensely, feel neutral toward others and even hold relatively positive views of parties that regularly cooperate with their preferred party.

The multiparty problem

A single average distance between in-party and out-party evaluations can conceal substantial heterogeneity. Two voters may receive the same polarization score despite having very different patterns of partisan affect.

Multiparty systems do not provide voters with a single out-party, but with a structured set of political alternatives.

This matters because the relationship between parties is itself political. Coalition partners, ideological neighbours and direct competitors may all be evaluated differently.

Comparability across countries

Cross-national comparisons introduce another complication. Party systems vary in size, ideological structure, coalition patterns and electoral relevance. An average out-party evaluation in a four-party system is not necessarily equivalent to the same quantity in a highly fragmented system.

Researchers therefore face a choice between measures that are easy to compare and measures that preserve more of the political structure of each party system.

Dyadic approaches

One possible solution is to treat evaluations as relational: rather than collapsing all out-parties into a single score, analyse specific voter-party or party-dyad relationships.

This makes it possible to ask whether affect varies according to politically meaningful relationships, such as ideological proximity, coalition cooperation or government-opposition status.

Measurement as a substantive choice

There is therefore no purely technical solution. The appropriate measure depends on the substantive question. If the goal is to capture broad hostility between partisan camps, aggregation may be useful. If the goal is to understand how particular interparty relationships shape affect, a more disaggregated approach may be preferable.

In comparative research, measurement choices should therefore reflect not only statistical convenience, but also the political structure being studied.
