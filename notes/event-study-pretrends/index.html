What should we make of significant pre-trends?

A short note on how to interpret event-study diagnostics when pre-treatment coefficients suggest differential trends.

24 August 2026 5 min read
Causal Inference Event Studies

Event-study plots are often used as a visual diagnostic for assessing whether treated and comparison units appear to evolve similarly before treatment.

A common interpretation is straightforward: if the pre-treatment coefficients are statistically indistinguishable from zero, the design looks reassuring; if one or more coefficients are significant, the parallel-trends assumption may be in doubt.

Why significance alone is not enough

Statistical significance is useful, but it does not by itself tell us whether the observed divergence is substantively important. A very small pre-treatment difference may become statistically detectable in a large sample without representing a large violation in practical terms.

Pre-treatment estimates should be interpreted in terms of both statistical uncertainty and substantive magnitude.

The pattern also matters. A single coefficient that differs from zero may tell a different story from a sequence of coefficients showing a systematic upward or downward trend.

Comparing pre- and post-treatment patterns

Another useful comparison is between the size of pre-treatment divergences and the estimated change after treatment. If the post-treatment shift is much larger than the differences observed before treatment, the event study may still provide informative descriptive evidence.

That does not restore a clean causal interpretation. Once the identifying assumptions appear questionable, the strongest causal language should usually be avoided.

A more cautious interpretation

In practice, significant pre-trends may therefore change the interpretation of a result rather than make the analysis useless. The estimates can still describe how the treated and comparison groups evolve around the event, but they should be presented with appropriate caution.

This distinction is especially important when event studies are being used as robustness checks rather than as the main identification strategy.
