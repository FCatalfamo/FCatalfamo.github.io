Coalition negotiations are political events too

Why coalition bargaining may matter for citizens' political evaluations before a government is formally created.

1 September 2026 6 min read
Coalition Politics Political Behaviour

Coalition formation is often treated as a discrete political event: an election takes place, parties negotiate, a government is formed, and researchers then study how citizens respond to the new cabinet. But politically, the process is rarely so clean.

Negotiations may last days, weeks or even months. During that period, voters receive information about which parties are talking to one another, what compromises are being discussed and which coalitions appear increasingly likely.

Negotiations as political signals

One possibility is that coalition negotiations themselves provide voters with information about party relationships. If two parties repeatedly appear together in negotiations, voters may begin to perceive them as more politically compatible even before a coalition agreement is formally signed.

Coalition bargaining may therefore be part of the treatment, rather than simply the period preceding it.

This possibility matters especially for research designs that define government formation as a single treatment date. If voters already react during the bargaining period, estimates based on the formal cabinet start may understate or mis-time the actual political process.

A possible empirical implication

Consider a panel survey fielded during coalition negotiations. Respondents interviewed early in the fieldwork may still perceive the eventual coalition parties as competitors, while respondents interviewed later may already have observed repeated signals of cooperation.

In such cases, the relevant timing of political exposure may not correspond perfectly to the formal date on which the cabinet takes office.

Why this matters

More broadly, this suggests that political events that researchers code as discrete transitions may sometimes be better understood as processes. Coalition formation is one example: bargaining, announcements, agreements and cabinet formation may each provide distinct signals to voters.

This does not mean that every stage needs to be treated as a separate causal event. But it does suggest that the informational environment surrounding coalition negotiations deserves more attention when studying citizens' responses to government formation.
